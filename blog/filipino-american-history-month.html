Filipino American History Month: Why October Matters to Us

Published October 2026 · By Team Sinigang

We named our studio after a sour Filipino soup for a reason. Team Sinigang is a Filipino family: a dad, a mom, an 8-year-old, and an 18-year-old who just left for college nearby. October is Filipino American History Month, and as a Pinoy household making games in Los Angeles, it means a lot to us. Here's why it's in October, a few stories worth knowing, and how we're celebrating.

📅 Why October?

On October 18, 1587, the first recorded Filipinos in what's now the continental United States came ashore at Morro Bay, California. They were "Luzones Indios," sailors aboard the Spanish galleon Nuestra Señora de Esperanza. That's more than 430 years of Filipino history in America, a lot longer than most people realize.

October is also the birth month of labor leader Larry Itliong, and that's no coincidence either.

✊ Larry Itliong and the Delano Grape Strike

If you know Cesar Chavez, you should know Larry Itliong. In 1965, Filipino farmworkers led by Itliong walked out of the vineyards around Delano, California, and started the Delano Grape Strike. Chavez and his union soon joined them, and together they built one of the most important labor movements in American history.

🎂 October 25 is Larry Itliong Day

California now honors Itliong on his birthday. The Manongs, the generation of Filipino farmworkers who came before him and stood with him, deserve to be remembered right alongside him.

📚 History, Not Just Heritage

🙏 Honoring Fred & Dorothy Cordova

This month exists because of a husband-and-wife team from Seattle. In 1957, Fred and Dorothy Laigo Cordova started Filipino Youth Activities, a community group for Filipino kids built around sports, folk dance, and parade marching. In 1982 they founded the Filipino American National Historical Society (FANHS), with Fred as its first president and Dorothy as its executive director.

Fred called Filipino Americans "the forgotten Asian Americans" and wrote a book by that name. Dorothy spent years recording oral histories so ordinary Filipino American lives wouldn't be lost. FANHS keeps the National Pinoy Archives in Seattle to this day. Fred passed away in 2013. Every October, this month carries their legacy, whether people know their names or not. Now you do.

📹 Hear their story in their own words: the University of Washington's Seattle Civil Rights & Labor History Project has video interviews with the Cordovas, FANHS's founders page tells more, and their granddaughter Gennette Cordova shares family photos and a beautiful tribute in the South Seattle Emerald.

FANHS proposed the observance in 1991, and the first Filipino American History Month was celebrated in 1992. Congress recognized October as Filipino American History Month in 2009. FANHS deliberately calls it history month: it's about the real events and people who shaped America, not only food and festivals. (Although we'll happily talk food, too.)

That history includes the Filipino and Filipino American veterans of World War II, who received the Congressional Gold Medal in 2017, the farmworkers in the fields, and generations of nurses, sailors, and families who built lives here.

🍲 How We're Celebrating

⚔️ Play Lapu-Lapu: Defender of Mactan

Our most ambitious browser game is set during the real Battle of Mactan on April 27, 1521, when Datu Lapu-Lapu's warriors drove back Magellan's forces. It's Philippine history from before any Filipino reached California, and it's where a lot of our pride starts. Play it free.

🍋 Cook a pot of sinigang

The sour tamarind soup that named our studio. Our recipes page has sinigang sa sampalok, bulalo, nilaga, and tinolang manok. Serve hot with rice. Always.

📖 Learn a little more

The Filipino American National Historical Society is the best place to start. Ask your lolo or lola for their story, too. Some of the best history isn't written down yet.

☕ Out in the Community

We haven't just been writing about it. This weekend we went to a Filipino ube and mango festival, and the next day we turned it into a full Pinoy family day out.

First stop was Malaya, a Filipino-inspired coffee shop in Arcadia. I got the Malaya Signature latte, my wife got the double ube, and our 8-year-old picked the strawberry calamansi lemonade, a Filipino citrus classic, though he didn't know it. We also split two ensaymadas, mocha Nutella and chocolate pistachio, because of course we did.

Look closely at the window and you'll spot three signs in Baybayin, the pre-colonial Filipino script: Tahanan, Pamana, Pamilya. Home, legacy, family. That's Filipino American History Month in three words.

Then it was on to Max's, the Filipino restaurant famous for its fried chicken, for kare-kare, pancit canton, fried tofu, and garlic rice. It was so good we forgot to take a single picture. Classic Pinoy family move.
